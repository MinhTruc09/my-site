# Registration Drift

*Algorithmic philosophy behind the hero background (`hero-sketch.ts`).*

A risograph never prints the same sheet twice. Two drums, two inks and a sheet of paper that slips half a millimetre: that slippage is the soul of the print. **Registration Drift** treats the hero as a living print run. Every frame is a new sheet passing through a two-drum machine, and the image is what the machine's small imperfections leave behind. It is not a picture of a poster. It is the press itself, still running.

The image is made only of **halftone screens**. A cobalt screen is set at 15° and a signal-red screen at 75°, the angles real printers use to keep two inks from fighting. Each dot's area, not its radius, carries the tone, so the eye reads the value correctly from across the room. The cobalt tone is a slow field: a diagonal ramp that gathers ink toward the bottom-right, following the eye path of the reference posters, and is disturbed by low-frequency noise so that the density breathes like uneven ink on a drum. The red screen prints only one form: **the big circle**, the sun and lens of the moodboard, its edge dissolving into dots rather than ending on a line. Where the red overprints the blue it is multiplied, as real ink would be, and the overlap darkens to a wine. That is red meeting blue, enacted rather than illustrated.

Time is **counted, not flowing**. The press advances in discrete ticks, about twelve per second, and nothing interpolates between them. Fields step, stripes step, the circle steps. **Speed stripes** cut through the sun as stock-coloured bars snapped to the screen grid, in the way the FEVER letters are sliced. Each bar extends and retracts in whole cells, staggered like a chorus. Every few seconds the red drum slips: the red screen jumps a couple of pixels out of register for two ticks, then the machine corrects itself. The rhythm is staccato, mechanical and stamped. It never floats, never springs and never blurs.

The visitor's pointer acts like a hand resting on the press. The circle leans toward it a little, never more than a couple of cells, and it moves one cell per tick, as a carriage would rather than a cursor. Around the pointer the cobalt screen lifts slightly, as if a thumb had pressed the paper away from the drum. The reaction is deliberately small. The hero text belongs to the visitor; the press is only the room it sits in.

Each seed is a different plate: different noise, different stripe rhythm, a different moment of slippage. The same seed always prints the same sheet. Every constant (screen angles, cell size, tick rate, drift cadence) should feel as if it was set by a printer who has run ten thousand sheets and knows exactly when a misregistration is beautiful and when it is merely wrong. This is a meticulously tuned machine. Its imperfections are calibrated, and that calibration is the craft.
